Bread education / Mix and folds

Strength work · not a second bulk

Fold to organize. Stop before you argue with gas.

Mixing gets flour wet. Folds line gluten up while the dough is still young. Bulk fermentation is a different clock: rise, jiggle, aroma. A fold schedule that never ends is how bakers confuse strength with time.

Direct answer: you do not have to knead. Mix until there is no dry flour, then three or four gentle fold sets in the first couple of hours. When the dough holds together, leave it alone and read fermentation for when to shape.

Pick a method

A working schedule

Early folds, then hands off

  1. Mix to incorporated.
    Follow mix order. No dry flour. A shaggy start is allowed if a rest or folds will follow.
  2. Fold three or four times in the first two hours.
    About 30–45 minutes apart is a plan, not a law. Skip a set if the dough already feels organized.
  3. Let bulk finish without more wrestling.
    Shaping tension comes later on shaping batards. Extra folds on a gassy dough knock out the work fermentation already did.
Smallest useful kit: a tub, wet hands, and a note of how many fold sets you actually did.

Before you ask

Fold questions

Do I have to knead sourdough?

No. Mix until combined, then fold during bulk. Kneading is a valid shortcut for some doughs. It is not required for a good first loaf.

Stretch-and-fold or coil fold?

Stretch-and-fold suits dough that still has edges. Coil folds suit wetter dough that wants to be lifted and tucked as a mass. If it tears, you are late or too rough — see sticky dough.

How many folds is enough?

Enough that the dough holds a shape through the last fold. Three or four early sets is common. More is not automatically stronger; overworked dough tears and then goes slack.

Is a windowpane required?

A thin window can confirm gluten, but lean sourdough often shapes well without a perfect pane. Trust elasticity and a skin more than a single stretch test.
