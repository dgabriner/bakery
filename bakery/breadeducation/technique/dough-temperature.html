Bread education / Dough temperature

The mix has a temperature

The dough's heat is the clock you actually own.

Room temperature is a rumor. The mixed dough is the fact. A few degrees at mix can add or subtract an hour of bulk, change stickiness, and push a loaf from taut to slack. Water temperature is the lever. There is no single bakery number that fits every kitchen.

Direct answer: stick a thermometer in the dough after it comes together. If it is cooler than you meant, use warmer water next mix. If it is warmer, use cooler water or expect a shorter bulk. Write both numbers down.

The lever

Change water first

  1. Measure the mixed dough.
    Air, flour bag, and tap are inputs. The dough is the output. An instant-read thermometer belongs in the first-loaf kit.
  2. Use water as the fast adjustment.
    Warmer water raises dough temperature; cooler water lowers it. Ice water is a tool for a hot day, not a personality.
  3. Hold the formula while you learn the range.
    Two bakes at the same mix temperature teach you more than one clever equation. Desired dough temperature formulas exist; they help only after you have a target you actually like.
Smallest useful kit: an instant-read thermometer and a note of mix temperature beside bulk length.

Before you ask

Temperature questions

What dough temperature should I aim for?

There is no single correct number. Many lean doughs behave in a comfortably warm range, roughly the mid-70s Fahrenheit, but flour, inoculation, and schedule matter more than copying a bakery target. Pick a range, hit it twice, then adjust.

Why is my dough colder than the room?

Cool flour and cold tap water drag the mix down. In San Francisco that is common. Warm the water, not the whole apartment.

Can dough be too warm at mix?

Yes. Warm dough ferments faster, feels stickier, and can overshoot bulk while you are still folding. Cooler water next time, or a shorter warm phase.

Do I need a proofing box?

No. A thermometer and a note beat a box you do not read. Buy the thermometer first.
