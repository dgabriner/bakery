Bread education / Troubleshooting / Overproofed vs underproofed

Proof clinic · two clocks

Too early and too late can both bake short.

Under-proofed dough is tight, reluctant, and often dense. Over-proofed dough is slack, fragile, and often flat. A photograph of crumb without a timeline will mix them up. Name the stage — bulk or final proof — then trust feel over the clock.

Direct answer: if a gentle poke springs back fast and the dough feels taut, it is usually early. If the dent stays and the skin feels thin or shiny, it is usually late. If it never held a round even when young, read loaf spreads flat before you blame proofing.

Diagnosis table

Which side of ready are you on?

CueUsually underUsually over
PokeDent fills quickly. Dough feels tense.Dent stays. Dough feels fragile or wet.
ShapeTears, fights the fold, little give.Will not take a skin; smears; seams unzip.
Score and ovenBursts beside the cut; dense, dramatic spring that still looks tight inside.Score collapses; loaf sits or pancakes; little vertical spring.
CrumbTight, sometimes with tunnels high under the crust. See dense loaf.Uneven, fragile walls, or a loaf that looks open but has no structure. Often siblings of no oven spring.
AromaMild, doughy, not much tang.Sharp, boozy, or overly acidic; dough may also smell spent.

These patterns overlap. Warm dough, weak flour, and a sluggish starter can fake either side. Confirm with the process notes on fermentation.

Two stages

Bulk can be late while the basket looks fine

If bulk went too far

The dough is already slack before you shape. Extra folds will smear it. Shape gently, proof short, and bake. Next bake, catch bulk earlier or cool the dough. See dough temperature.

If only the final proof ran long

You had a decent bulk, then the basket slumped. Next bake, move to the fridge sooner or shorten the counter proof. The cold retard is a scheduling tool, not a rescue for dough that already spent itself.

If both stages were short

Expect a dense crumb and a loaf that still feels tight after baking. Extend warm bulk to a real jiggle before you add water or starter.

If the starter was not at peak

A quiet or past-peak culture can under-ferment the dough even on a long clock. Check when the starter is ready to mix.

If this, then that

One change for the next bake

  1. Write when it felt wrong.
    End of bulk, in the basket, on the peel, or after the score. That timestamp is the diagnosis.
  2. Do not add water and starter in the same bake.
    Pick bulk length, dough temperature, or inoculation — one of those.
  3. Keep shape and bake comparable.
    Otherwise you will not know whether proofing or the oven moved the result.
Smallest useful kit: a straight-sided tub or aliquot jar, a finger for the poke, and a note of dough temperature at mix.

Before you ask

Proof questions

Is overproofing the same as over-fermentation?

Almost, but name the stage. Bulk can go too far before you shape. Final proof can go too far in the basket after a good bulk. The poke, the skin, and when the dough went slack tell you which clock you missed.

Can a loaf be under-proofed and still spread?

Yes, if it is very wet and never got a skin. Tight, tearing dough that still pancakes is usually under-fermented plus weak tension or too much water, not a loaf that spent itself.

Does the poke test work on cold dough?

It is quieter. Cold dough springs back more slowly even when it is ready. Combine the poke with how the loaf looks in the basket and whether it still holds a score.

Should I add more starter if the dough is under-proofed?

Not as a first move. More starter speeds the next bake; it does not repair this one. Finish this loaf, then change dough temperature or bulk length next time.
