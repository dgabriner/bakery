Bread education / Troubleshooting / Starter not rising

Starter rescue · read the jar

A quiet starter is not a failed starter.

If it is not climbing the jar after a feeding, do not assume it is dead. First decide whether you are still establishing a new culture or maintaining an established one. Then read temperature, feeding ratio, flour, hydration, and peak timing together. There is no universal feeding schedule that fits every kitchen.

Direct answer: wait when the jar is new, cool, or recently overfed; intervene when an established culture has no repeatable peak, smells harshly acidic, or has been starved. Change one variable at a time.

A starter can smell sharper when it is hungry, but smell alone is not a safety test. Visible mold or unusual pink or orange growth is the clear stop sign.

If this, then that

Temperature, flour, and hydration change the picture

If the kitchen is cool

San Francisco counters often sit well below a warm bakery bench. A culture that peaked in six hours in July can look asleep in January. Find a stable, comfortably warm spot — not a heating pad, not a hot stove, not a sealed oven with leftover heat. Steadiness matters more than forcing speed.

If you just changed flour

All-purpose, bread flour, whole wheat, and rye do not rise the same height. Rye and whole wheat often look busier; a white-flour starter can look modest and still be strong. Give a flour change a few comparable feedings before calling it a failure. The starter science page explains why rye can look explosive.

If the starter is very wet

A loose batter can bubble hard and still fail to climb. A stiffer mix (less water, same flour) often shows height more honestly. Hydration is a dial, not a moral category.

If you feed by the clock only

Feeding a starter that has not peaked yet can look like “it never rises.” Feeding one that collapsed hours ago can look like “it is always flat.” Mark the jar. Peak is the dome, the first wrinkle, the moment the top begins to relax — then feed, or build a levain from that window.

A restrained rescue

Change one variable for three feeds

  1. Mark the jar at feeding.
    A rubber band turns a vague impression into a rise, a peak, and a fall you can compare.
  2. Keep the meal and container consistent.
    If every feed changes flour, water, jar, and place on the counter, the starter cannot teach you which change mattered. There is no single correct ratio — 1:1:1 and 1:5:5 are both tools. Pick one and hold it.
  3. Only then change warmth, ratio, or a share of whole grain.
    Write down bubbles, height, aroma, and when the top begins to relax. The repeatable pattern is the rescue, not a heroic extra feeding.
Smallest useful kit: a straight-sided jar, a rubber band or tape mark, a scale, and one consistent feeding plan for the next few observations.

Before you ask

Starter questions

Does a healthy starter have to double?

No. Jar shape, hydration, and flour change the height. A repeatable peak window is more useful than a single target.

How do I tell overfeeding from underfeeding?

A tiny seed in a large meal can look dead for many hours and then move late. A large seed in a small meal often peaks quickly, turns sharp, and then sits. Keep the same ratio for a few cycles and write down time-to-peak instead of feeding by the clock alone.

What does liquid on top mean?

Often that it has used much of its food. Stir or pour it off, refresh it consistently, then watch the next full cycle. Liquid alone is not a safety test.

When should I throw it away?

Fuzzy growth or pink or orange discoloration means discard it. A sharp, hungry, or boozy smell is usually a schedule signal. When you are unsure about growth, do not bake with it.
