Bread education / Sourdough / Starter ready to mix

Peak window · not a personality test

Mix while the jar is still telling the truth.

An established starter is ready when it has a peak you can repeat: it rises, it domes, it smells lively, and it has not yet collapsed into a sharp puddle. That window is yours. It is not a universal number of hours, and it is not a requirement to double in a particular jar.

Direct answer: feed, mark the line, and mix (or build the levain) at or just after the high point. If the jar never moved, do not mix — use starter not rising. If it peaked and fell hours ago, refresh and catch the next window.

Levain versus jar

The mix is a timed ingredient

Using starter straight from the jar

Works when the jar itself is at peak and your dough schedule matches that peak. Count the flour and water in the starter toward the formula.

Building a levain

A levain is a portion you feed for the bake so the peak meets mix time. Same cues: rise, dome, aroma. The sourdough page shows example builds — they are plans, not laws.

Float test

A spoonful in water can support the story. It cannot replace the marked jar. Stiff starters and whole-grain starters often lie on this test.

New cultures

Week-one jars are still learning. Follow starter day one until the rhythm repeats, then come back here.

A restrained habit

Three feeds teach you more than one trick

  1. Mark the jar at feeding.
    Same flour, same ratio, same place, for a few cycles so the hours-to-peak become a number you own.
  2. Mix from the dome, not from the alarm clock.
    A cool San Francisco counter stretches the window. A warm week shortens it. Write the hours down.
  3. If you miss it, refresh — do not panic-mix.
    A past-peak jar in a large dough is how sharp, slack bakes get made.
Smallest useful kit: a straight-sided jar, a rubber band, and the same feeding plan you used yesterday.

Before you ask

Readiness questions

Does the starter have to double before I mix?

No. Jar shape, hydration, and flour change the height. A repeatable peak window on your plan matters more than a universal doubling.

Is the float test required?

Optional evidence, not a law. Gassy paste can float past its best; a stiff starter can fail to float and still raise dough. Trust the rise-and-fall you marked.

Can I mix with a starter that has already fallen?

You can, but the dough often runs faster, tastes sharper, and may weaken. Recent dip: maybe. Hours-old collapse: refresh and catch the next peak.

What if the starter is not rising at all?

Different job. Use the starter not rising clinic before you put a quiet jar into a loaf.
