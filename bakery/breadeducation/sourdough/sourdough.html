Bread education / Sourdough

Culture · levain · readiness

Make the culture predictable enough to teach you.

Sourdough is not a personality test for your starter. It is a living process you can make more legible by logging feed ratio, flour, temperature, rise, aroma, and time to peak.

Core idea: build the levain for the schedule you actually have, then use the dough's rise and strength—not an inherited clock—to decide when to move.

The working model

Starter → levain → dough → record

01 · Maintain

Feed for activity

Track the ratio, flour blend, temperature, and time to peak. A culture that doubles at a known pace is easier to schedule than one judged by age or smell alone.

02 · Build

Match the bake

A levain is the portion you build for a loaf. Count its flour and water in total flour and hydration, and choose a build that peaks when you plan to mix.

03 · Observe

Read the dough

During bulk, look for volume gain, bubbles, elasticity, aroma, and a gentle wobble. Temperature changes the pace; the dough supplies the evidence.

A deeper working notebook

What to log when a starter is not behaving

It rises quickly, then collapses

That is activity, not necessarily peak strength. Record the time from feeding to dome, the time to flattening, and whether the smell moves from milky/yeasty to sharply acidic. Build the levain before the culture has exhausted itself, or increase the feed ratio for a longer window.

It barely rises

Check temperature, flour freshness, feed ratio, and whether the retained seed is already very acidic. Refresh with a smaller inoculation, use some whole-grain or rye flour for a few feeds, and give the culture several cycles before judging it.

It smells sharp or solvent-like

The culture may be hungry or past peak. Do not “fix” it by changing every variable at once. Feed by weight, keep the jar at a stable temperature, and wait for a clean rise-and-fall pattern.

It rises but the dough does not

A starter can trap gas in a jar and still be poorly matched to a large dough. Compare the levain's peak time with the dough's temperature and inoculation. The bake formula, flour strength, and bulk endpoint still matter.

Food-safety boundary: discard cultures with fuzzy mold, pink/orange discoloration, or putrid odors. A sharp sour smell alone is not a diagnosis; appearance, sanitation, and context matter.

Build math

Three levain builds, three different schedules

BuildExampleUseful when
Fast, small build10 g seed + 25 g flour + 25 g waterA same-day mix and a warm room. Peak arrives quickly; watch it closely.
Balanced build10 g seed + 50 g flour + 50 g waterA typical overnight or workday schedule. A 1:5:5 build gives more food and a wider window.
Stiff build10 g seed + 70 g flour + 40 g waterWhen you want a firmer levain that is easier to shape into a dough, or when your formula already carries high water.

These are planning examples, not universal prescriptions. Use the formula page to account for the build's flour and water in the final dough.

Sensory calibration

Learn your starter with a repeatable tasting panel

Rise heightTime to peakAromaSurfaceTextureCollapse time

For three feedings, use the same jar, flour, ratio, water, and room. Mark the level after feeding. At 2-hour intervals, record height, bubbles, aroma, and whether the top is domed, flat, or receding. This gives you a culture profile that is more useful than “my starter looks active.”

Then build a levain and record the same cues. The levain is not simply a larger starter; it is a timed ingredient whose peak should meet the dough's schedule.
