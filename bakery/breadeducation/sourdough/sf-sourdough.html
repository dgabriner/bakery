Bread education / SF sourdough

Culture · Climate · Patience

The loaf this city named.

San Francisco sourdough is not a recipe. It is a relationship between a wild culture and a cool coastal city — and we bake inside that relationship every morning. Here is what it means on our bench, and what it can mean on yours.

Smallest useful kit: a healthy starter, a jar with room to double, a dough thermometer, and the patience to let a cooler room have its say.

The name on the tin

What “San Francisco sourdough” actually means

Long before sourdough was trendy, this city's bakers were famous for loaves raised by wild yeast and lactic acid bacteria working together — a partnership so tied to this place that one of its bacteria carries the city's name. The tang people associate with San Francisco comes from that partnership: yeasts lift the dough while bacteria build the acids that taste like home.

Two honest consequences follow. First, you do not need San Francisco tap water or fog to make great sourdough — the method travels. Second, what made these loaves famous was never a secret ingredient. It was a culture kept alive daily and a baker willing to ferment slowly in a cool room. Both of those are learnable, and both are exactly what the rest of this page trains.

Our starters

A culture is a pet, not a pantry item

Ratio beats age

An old starter is not automatically a good one. Feeding ratio, flour blend, temperature, and time-to-peak matter far more than the birthday. Ours are managed by the same checks we teach: doubled volume, domed top, pleasant tang.

Peak is a window

A starter is ready when its activity matches your dough schedule — domed and bubbly, floating in water, smelling pleasantly sour-yeasty. Past peak it turns sharp and slack. Learn the window in the four states.

Count the levain

The portion you build for a bake brings its own flour and water. Include them in hydration math or every comparison you make will lie to you. The full accounting lives on Formula.

Bakery truth: production starters and home starters obey the same rules — we just feed ours in bigger buckets. When yours misbehaves, refresh, warm slightly, and observe before changing everything at once.

Reading our climate

What the fog does to your dough

San Francisco's cool, damp air is a slow-fermentation machine. Where a warm kitchen might finish bulk in three hours, a foggy Mission morning stretches the same dough longer — and that extra time is where flavor compounds develop.

Cool rooms are an asset

Every 8 °F drop roughly slows fermentation, buying you deeper flavor before the dough over-ferments. Use it: longer, cooler bulk for complexity; warm the spot if you need speed. Measure the dough, not the room.

Schedule around signs, not clocks

In this city a recipe's “4-hour bulk” is a rumor. Watch rise percentage, jiggle, and the poke test instead — the cues taught on Fermentation. Write down what your kitchen did; your house has its own weather.

Flour choices

Feed the culture like you mean it

Strength first

Bread flour around 12–13% protein builds the gluten network that holds an open crumb through a long, cool ferment.

Whole grain wakes everything up

Bran and germ carry enzymes and flavor; they accelerate fermentation and drink more water. Add them in stages and expect the dough to change character — see Whole grain.

Freshness is flavor

Fresh-milled flour is thirstier and livelier. Autolyse longer and expect faster rises; treat it as a feature, not a problem.

Troubleshooting lens

Sourdough problems, read honestly

Too sour, too fast

Over-fermentation, usually warmth. Use starter at peak, shorten bulk, and bake before the dough smells sharply acidic.

Not sour at all, and bland

Under-fermentation. Extend bulk until the dough jiggles and passes the poke test; cooler rooms simply need more patience.

Sluggish starter

A diagnostic signal, not a death sentence. Refresh on a strict schedule, warm the jar slightly, and watch the rise pattern before changing flour or ratios all at once.
