Bread education / Pan dulce

Sweet dough · Morning counters · Family tables

The sweet breads deserve real teaching too.

Conchas, cuernos, orejas, picon, cortadillos — pan dulce is a family of enriched doughs with its own logic. Most bread education stops at sourdough; this page teaches the sweet counter the same honest way we teach every loaf.

Smallest useful kit: a stand mixer or strong arms, butter soft enough to blend, a scale, and the patience to let enriched dough take its time.

The family

One dough, many mornings

Walk any panaderia counter and you are mostly looking at variations on a theme: a soft, enriched dough — flour, eggs, milk, sugar, butter — shaped, topped, and finished differently. Learn the base dough once and the whole case starts reading like dialects of one language.

Conchas

The shell-topped classic: a soft ball of dough under a cookie-like paste, scored into ridges that crack open in the oven. The topping is sugar, butter, and flour — a short dough sitting on a rich one.

Cuernos & orejas

Laminated-adjacent relatives built on butter and layers: croissant-style horns and palmier ears, where fold-and-roll technique matters as much as the dough itself.

Picon & the filled cousins

Picon carries its sweetness in a dense, pudding-like filling; cortadillos and colchon slice from sheet trays; budin and barras round out the tray. Same principles, different geometry.

The science

Enrichment changes everything

Sugar, milk, eggs, and butter are what make pan dulce taste like celebration — and each one rewires fermentation and browning. This is standard enriched-dough science applied daily on our pan dulce line.

Fat tenderizes

Butter and egg yolks shorten gluten strands, giving that tender, close crumb. Knead longer than lean dough to rebuild strength, and handle gently so you do not paste the layers shut.

Sugar slows, then browns

Sugar ties up water and slows yeast, which is why sweet doughs often want more time or yeasts bred to tolerate them. The same sugar browns fast — color, not clock, decides doneness.

Practical bridge: if sourdough taught you to read gas and strength, pan dulce teaches temperature and restraint. Keep notes exactly like an SF Baker journal wants: room temp, mix feel, proof length, oven position, result.

Hands-on

Baking conchas at home, honestly

  1. Mix the base dough.
    Flour, eggs, milk, sugar, butter, yeast, salt to a smooth, supple dough. Enriched dough rewards thorough mixing after hydration.
  2. Bulk patiently.
    Expect slower rise than lean dough — the sugar is working against the yeast. Judge by volume and feel, not the recipe's hour count.
  3. Portion and top.
    Round balls on parchment. Flatten a disc of cookie paste over each, then score the shell pattern — a bench knife's straight edge makes clean ridges.
  4. Proof to pillowy.
    Enriched dough proofs until puffy and light, not doubled-and-dramatic. Gentle indent that springs back slowly means load the tray.
  5. Bake for color, cool for texture.
    Sugar browns early: pull when the topping is golden at the ridges, not dark. Cool enough to set — warm pan dulce tears; rested pan dulce peels like it should.
