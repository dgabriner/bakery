Bread education / Fermentation

Time · temperature · strength

Stop asking the clock to do the dough's job.

Fermentation is a moving target. Temperature, inoculation, flour, hydration, salt, and dough strength all shift the pace. A reliable baker learns to mark the dough and recognize the signs.

Practice: put a straight-sided sample of dough or an aliquot jar beside the main dough. Mark the starting level and photograph it when you mix, fold, shape, and bake.

A readable sequence

What to notice at each stage

Bulk

Strength + gas

The dough moves from shaggy to smoother, more elastic, and gently aerated. Folds build strength while preserving gas. End bulk when the dough has enough rise and structure to shape.

Final proof

Expansion after shaping

Shaping creates a skin that holds the loaf. Proof until the dough feels aerated and springs back slowly. Under-proofing is tight; over-proofing is fragile.

Temperature

Speed control

Warmth accelerates fermentation; cool rooms and refrigeration slow it. Measure dough temperature when possible, not just the air.

Flavor

Time with boundaries

Longer, cooler fermentation can deepen flavor, but time only helps while the dough retains enough strength to hold its shape.

One-variable practice

Run three controlled bakes

  1. Hold the formula.
    Use the same flour, water, salt, leaven, and shape.
  2. Change temperature.
    Move the dough 4–6°F and record time to the same visual cue.
  3. Change inoculation.
    Keep dough temperature stable and compare bulk length, flavor, and strength.

Return to the observation states and bulk stages on the learning hub after each bake.

Fermentation diagnosis

Use the baked crumb as evidence, not a verdict

PatternOften points towardConfirm with
Tight crumb with tunnels high under the crustUnder-fermentation or insufficient final proof.Bulk rise, dough temperature, starter peak, and whether the loaf had exaggerated oven spring.
Flat loaf, weak score, fragile doughOver-fermentation, weak flour, excessive acidity, or insufficient strength.Shape retention, dough feel, bulk percentage, and whether the dough was difficult to score without collapse.
Large holes with dense bands betweenUneven gas distribution, under-fermentation, or shaping that left large bubbles in place.Pre-shape degassing, fold pattern, proofing time, and cross-section—not just the largest hole.
Gummy centerUnder-baking, a very large loaf, excessive hydration, or slicing before cooling.Internal temperature, bake time after the crust colors, loaf size, and cooling duration.
Important: crumb patterns overlap. A photograph without formula, temperature, and process notes cannot reliably distinguish under-proofing from weak dough or an oven problem.

Temperature discipline

Measure the dough, then write the target

At mix

Record flour, water, room, and dough temperature. A warmer final dough may require less starter or a shorter bulk.

During bulk

Log temperature after folds and mark the dough level. A clear container or aliquot jar turns “looks ready” into a traceable observation.

Before bake

Record whether the shaped dough was room-temperature proofed or cold-retarded, and how firm it felt at scoring.
