Bread education / Pan dulce family / Picon

Crown of crunch

Where the topping IS the pastry.

Where a concha wears a thin scored sheet, picon wears architecture: a thick, chunky sugar crust domed over soft enriched bread — crackle against cloud in every bite.

The recipe card

Picon in grams

Dough (master base)Crunchy cap
Flour 500 g · milk 230 g · sugar 90 g · butter 100 g · egg 1 · yeast 7 g · salt 10 gFlour 120 g · powdered sugar 120 g · cold butter 90 g · vanilla — rubbed to wet-sand clumps, chilled firm
  1. Knead & bulk.
    Master base kneaded smooth; bulk warm ~90 min until puffed.
  2. Divide 100 g, round tight.
    Surface tension is the shelf your crown sits on.
  3. Seat chilled caps.
    Press clumpy topping into thick domes; lay gently onto rounds.
  4. Proof 40–50 min.
    Visibly puffy under the cap — never doubled.
  5. Bake 350°F, 18–22 min.
    Crackled tops, golden bottoms. Cool so crunch sets.

Why the cap stays crisp: fat-coated flour resists gluten formation, so the rub bakes into shortbread-like rubble instead of merging with the dough.

Before you ask

Picon questions

How is picon different from a concha?

Same family, different cap: conchas wear a flat scored cookie sheet, while picon carries a thicker unscored dome that bakes into a chunky crunchy crown — the name nods to its rough, pocked look.

My topping sank into the dough. What happened?

The cap went on before rounding tightened the dough, or proof ran long and gas pushed through. Round firmly, seat the dome gently, and bake once visibly puffy — not doubled.

Can I flavor the topping?

Cinnamon, cocoa, or lime zest worked into the rub are classic. Keep total fat constant so the crumble texture survives.

Do picon keep their crunch overnight?

Some softening is inevitable as moisture migrates; store in a paper bag and re-crisp five minutes at 300°F. Sealed plastic preserves softness but surrenders the snap.
