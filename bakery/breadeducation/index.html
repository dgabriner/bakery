Fresh Loaf debrief

What a durable bread community keeps teaching

The Fresh Loaf is more than a recipe archive. Its lessons, forum threads, blog bakes, and comments form a long-running record of how bakers learn: make a loaf, describe what happened, compare variables, and bake again.

01 · Start simple

Process before perfection

The site's beginner lessons begin with a plain loaf and add complexity only after the fundamentals are visible. We preserve that order: mix, ferment, shape, bake, then vary.

02 · Measure honestly

Formula before anecdote

A useful bake record names flour, water, salt, leaven, dough weight, and timing. Baker's percentages make a formula scalable; preferment flour and water must be counted when comparing hydration.

03 · Read the dough

Signs beat the clock

Across beginner questions and advanced bake logs, rise, bubbles, elasticity, temperature, and jiggle are more portable than a fixed number of hours. Schedule is a plan; dough is the evidence.

04 · Keep the feedback loop

Show the whole bake

The strongest community posts pair a formula with process notes and photos of the loaf and crumb. A question becomes answerable when another baker can see what changed.

05 · Use your hands

Tools support judgment

A scale, thermometer, scraper, and simple bowl remove guesswork, but hand feel still teaches strength, extensibility, and readiness. Machines can speed a known process; they cannot replace observation.

06 · Hold advice lightly

Experience is not a guarantee

Forum answers are valuable evidence, not universal law. Flour, room temperature, starter culture, oven, and desired bread all change the result. Test one variable and keep a record.

Your path

Five steps to your first loaf

Follow this sequence once, then repeat it until the signs feel familiar. Each step builds on the last — there is no shortcut around watching the dough.

1

Feed

Refresh your starter 4–8 hours before mixing. It should at least double, smell pleasantly tangy, and pass the float test at peak.

2

Watch

Notice peak activity — domed top, fine bubbles at the sides, a slight sour-sweet aroma. This is your fermentation window opening.

3

Mix

Combine flour, water, and starter. Mix until no dry spots remain. Autolyse 20–40 minutes if your schedule allows — the dough will thank you.

4

Rest + fold

Let the dough rest, then fold every 30–45 minutes for 2–3 rounds. Watch it gain strength, smoothness, and a gentle jiggle.

5

Shape + bake

Pre-shape, rest 15 minutes, final shape, proof, score, and bake hot. Listen for the crackle — your dough is speaking.

The framework

Five factors that shape every loaf

When something goes wrong — or wonderfully right — one of these five is usually responsible. Learn to name them and you can adjust on purpose instead of guessing.

01

Time

Fermentation duration, rest intervals, and proof length. Too little = tight and bland; too much = slack and sour.

02

Temperature

Dough temp and ambient warmth control yeast and bacteria speed. A few degrees changes everything — measure the dough, not just the room.

03

Hydration

Water-to-flour ratio sets crumb openness, handling feel, and bake time. Higher hydration = more extensibility and a wetter bench.

04

Ratio

Starter percentage, salt level, and inoculation rate. More starter = faster fermentation; less = longer, deeper flavor.

05

Food

Flour type, freshness, and protein content feed the culture and structure. Whole grain accelerates fermentation and absorbs more water.

Culture check

Is your starter ready?

Before every bake, run through these quick checks. A healthy starter makes everything downstream easier — mixing, folding, shaping, all of it.

  • Rise: At least doubles (triples is great) within 4–8 hours at room temp after feeding.
  • Smell: Pleasantly tangy and yeasty — not nail polish, not pure vinegar, not gym socks.
  • Float test: A teaspoon of starter floats in water at peak. If it sinks, feed again and wait.
  • Surface: Domed and bubbly at peak; flat or receding means it passed peak — feed and restart timing.
  • Consistency: Thick batter or soft peak — adjust hydration at feeding if it's too stiff or soupy.

Commercial yeast + preferments

Yeasted bread follows the same living logic

Sourdough and commercial yeast are different tools, not different worlds. Both ferment flour, create gas, develop flavor, and respond to time and temperature. The main difference is how predictable the leavening is.

A

Active dry vs. instant

Active dry yeast is commonly dissolved in warm water first. Instant yeast can usually be mixed directly into the flour and is a little faster. Follow the package directions, then judge the dough rather than forcing a clock.

B

Preferments buy flavor

A poolish (equal flour and water) or biga (stiffer preferment) lets a small amount of yeast work over time. Count its flour and water in the final formula, just as you count flour and water in a levain.

C

Enrichment changes behavior

Sugar, milk, eggs, and butter soften crumb and add flavor, but they also slow yeast and brown the crust. Sweet, enriched doughs may need more time or yeast designed to tolerate higher sugar.

D

Salt is structure and control

Salt seasons the loaf, strengthens gluten, and slows fermentation. Forgetting it can make dough race ahead and taste flat. Weigh it with the flour instead of treating it as an afterthought.

E

Proofing is the final rise

After shaping, proof until the dough feels aerated and springs back slowly. Under-proofing gives a tight crumb; over-proofing gives weak structure and a loaf that deflates when scored.

F

Same journal, different leaven

Record yeast type or preferment, dough temperature, bulk time, proof time, and bake result. A commercial-yeast batch deserves the same careful record as a sourdough batch.

Practical bridge: If a sourdough dough is teaching you to read fermentation, a yeasted dough is a useful control. Keep flour, hydration, salt, and shaping similar; change only the leaven and compare the timing and flavor.

Bulk fermentation

The dough's story in four stages

During bulk fermentation your dough transforms from a shaggy mix into a living, stretchy mass. Watch for these milestones — they tell you when to shape, not the clock alone.

1 · Shaggy mix

Just combined. Weak, tears easily, sticky. No structure yet — this is normal. Do not rush to add flour.

2 · Smoothing

After folds, surface gets smoother. Dough holds shape briefly. Bubbles forming at edges. Gluten is organizing.

3 · Jiggly & airy

Noticeable rise, wobble when shaken, visible gas pockets. Gluten is strong and extensible — handle gently.

4 · Ready to shape

30–50% volume increase, domed profile, slight spring-back when gently pressed. Don't wait for it to collapse.

Reading signs

What fermentation looks like

Your starter and dough move through recognizable phases. Match what you see to these four states — they are more reliable than any timer on the counter.

Q

Quiet

Flat surface, few bubbles, mild smell. Just fed or still waking up. Wait — or warm the environment slightly.

B

Building

Bubbles appearing, volume increasing, tangy aroma developing. Fermentation is active — good time to mix if peak is near.

P

Lively (peak)

Domed, doubled or tripled, fine bubbles throughout, pleasant sour-yeasty smell. Peak activity — use it now.

F

Fading

Surface flattening or sinking, sharper acidic smell, liquidity at edges. Past peak — feed again before baking.

Hands-on

Shape, score, steam, and bake

Bulk fermentation tells you when the dough is ready. These steps turn that readiness into a finished loaf — tense on the outside, open on the inside.

Pre-shape

Turn dough out gently. Round or oval into a loose bundle to begin building surface tension. Rest 15–20 minutes uncovered on the bench — the gluten will relax just enough for final shaping.

Bench rest

Short pause between pre-shape and final shape. Dough spreads slightly and becomes easier to stretch. If it springs back aggressively, rest a few minutes longer.

Final shape

Stitch and tighten into a batard or boule. The goal is a smooth, taut skin without degassing the interior. A well-shaped loaf holds its form in the banneton.

Scoring

One confident slash ¼–½ inch deep at a shallow angle. Scoring controls expansion — too shallow and the loaf may burst; too deep and it can flatten. A lame or sharp razor works best.

Steam

Steam in the first 15–20 minutes keeps the crust elastic so the loaf can spring. Use a Dutch oven lid, tray of hot water, or ice cubes in a preheated pan — pick one method and stay consistent.

Bake

475°F (245°C) for 20 minutes covered or with steam, then 425°F (220°C) for 20–25 minutes until deeply colored. Internal temp around 206–210°F (96–99°C). Cool completely before slicing.

Deep reference

Baker's guide

Expand any topic below for practical notes. Then use the experiment planner to connect a problem to a specific adjustment — one variable at a time.

Time — fermentation windows

Bulk fermentation at 75°F (24°C) typically runs 3–5 hours for a standard loaf. Cold retard in the fridge adds 8–18 hours of flavor development. If your dough is ready before your schedule, refrigerate to pause. If it's slow, extend time before adding more starter.

Temperature — the invisible accelerator

Every 8°F (4°C) change roughly doubles or halves fermentation speed. Use a probe thermometer on the dough itself — ambient room temp is misleading. Target 75–78°F for predictable bulk. Below 68°F, expect long ferments; above 82°F, watch for over-proofing.

Hydration — feel and crumb

Lower hydration (58–65%) yields tighter crumb and easier handling — great for rolls and sandwich loaves. Mid range (68–75%) is the sourdough sweet spot. High hydration (78–85%) gives open crumb and ciabatta-like texture but demands confident handling.

Ratio — starter, salt, inoculation

Starter at 15–25% of total flour is common. Higher inoculation speeds fermentation; lower gives more flavor complexity over longer times. Salt at 2% of flour weight strengthens gluten and slows fermentation — never skip it or add it directly to your starter.

Food — flour choices

Bread flour (12–13% protein) gives strength. All-purpose works for softer crumb. Whole wheat and rye ferment faster and absorb more water — increase hydration or reduce starter when adding them. Freshly milled flour is thirsty; autolyse longer.

Folds — when and how

Stretch-and-folds or coil folds every 30–45 minutes during the first 2 hours of bulk build strength without degassing. Stop when the dough feels smooth, holds tension, and jiggles as a unit — usually 3–4 sets. Over-folding can tear gluten.

12-stage fermentation map

1–3: Mix, autolyse, first fold. 4–6: Active rise, regular folds, warming aroma. 7–9: Volume gain, jiggle test passes, gas visible. 10: Pre-shape. 11: Bench rest + final shape. 12: Final proof, poke test, bake. Track where you are on this map each bake to build intuition.

Build the habit

Keep a bake journal

The bakers who improve fastest are the ones who write things down — even a few lines per bake. Note your starter peak time, dough temp at mix, bulk duration, and how the crumb looked. Patterns emerge quickly.

SF Baker is our free community baking journal, open to every baker: track starters, formulas, and batches in the SF Baker portal. It is built for the path toward 1,000 loaves — logging each bake helps you see what changed when results shift.

Whether you use a notebook, a notes app, or the portal, the practice is the same: observe, record, adjust one factor, bake again.

Terms

Glossary

Quick definitions for language you'll hear in the bakery and see throughout this page.

Baker's percentage

A way to express recipes where flour always equals 100% and every other ingredient is a percentage of that flour weight — not of total dough. It makes scaling and comparison straightforward.

Bulk fermentation

The first rise after mixing, before shaping. The dough ferments as one mass in a bowl or tub. Most flavor development and gas production happen here.

Levain

Another word for the portion of starter that goes into your dough — your leavening culture. Often built at a specific ratio a few hours before mixing.

Autolyse

A rest period after combining flour and water (before salt and starter). It lets flour hydrate and gluten begin organizing, which can reduce mix time and improve extensibility.

Oven spring

The final burst of rise in the hot oven as gas expands and steam pushes the loaf upward. Good oven spring depends on adequate fermentation and strong surface tension from shaping.

Proof

The final rise after shaping, before baking. Also called final fermentation. The poke test — a gentle finger indent that springs back slowly — helps judge readiness.

Retard

Slow fermentation in a cold environment, usually the refrigerator. Extends flavor development and lets you bake on your schedule rather than the dough's.
